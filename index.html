Overview

The John Muir Trail is a 211-mile long-distance trail through the heart of the Sierra Nevada mountain range in California, widely regarded as one of the most spectacular wilderness routes in the world. Running from Yosemite Valley to the summit of Mt. Whitney, the highest peak in the contiguous United States at 14,505 feet, the trail passes through three national parks (Yosemite, Kings Canyon, and Sequoia), multiple wilderness areas, and some of the most breathtaking alpine scenery on the planet.


The JMT follows the Sierra crest closely, spending the majority of its length above 8,000 feet with ten major high passes, four of them above 12,000 feet. Hikers traverse crystalline alpine lakes, ancient granite domes, roaring Sierra rivers, lush flower-filled meadows, and ancient groves. It shares much of its route with the Pacific Crest Trail, meaning excellent trail infrastructure and well-established camping areas.


Most hikers travel southbound from Yosemite Valley (Happy Isles trailhead) to Mt. Whitney Portal, completing the trail in 17–21 days at a comfortable pace of 10–13 miles per day. Experienced thru-hikers and trail runners have completed it in under 14 days. The trail is well-marked and maintained, but its remoteness, altitude, and sustained physical demand make it a serious undertaking requiring careful preparation.

Permits & Planning

Yosemite Wilderness Permit (Southbound from Happy Isles): Required for overnight travel in Yosemite. Issued via a lottery system that opens in late March for the coming season at recreation.gov. Trailhead quota is small (roughly 12–25/day per trailhead). Day-of cancellations are released online at 11am PT. Set an alarm.

Alternative Start: Donohue Pass Area (Lyell Canyon): Fewer applications compete for permits at the Lyell Canyon trailhead, which bypasses Yosemite Valley entirely. You join the main JMT near the southern Yosemite boundary, a good strategy for securing a permit without the Happy Isles lottery odds.

Northbound from Whitney Portal: Requires a Mt. Whitney Zone permit from the Inyo National Forest lottery (opens February 1 at recreation.gov). The quota is 100 overnight hikers per day. Significantly better odds than Happy Isles.

Bear Canister: Legally required throughout the entire trail. Popular options include the BearVault BV500, Bearikade Weekender, and Garcia Backpacker's Cache. A full 7-day supply of food will fit in a 650-700 cubic inch canister for most hikers.

Leave No Trace: Camp at established sites or on durable surfaces 200 feet from water. All human waste must be packed out in wag bags in the Mt. Whitney Zone (above 10,400 ft). Fires are prohibited throughout most of the JMT corridor.

Route Breakdown

1
Yosemite Valley → Tuolumne Meadows
Begin at Happy Isles, climbing steadily through Little Yosemite Valley past the Vernal and Nevada Falls junction. The trail gains significant elevation quickly. Pass the Half Dome junction (a popular side trip adding 4–5 miles round-trip) before reaching the subalpine expanse of Tuolumne Meadows, a classic first camp or resupply point.
21 miles 4,200 ft gain Resupply: Tuolumne Store
2
Tuolumne Meadows → Reds Meadow
Cross Donohue Pass (11,056 ft), the southern boundary of Yosemite into the Ansel Adams Wilderness. Descend through the volcanic Mammoth Lakes region to Reds Meadow, home to the famous Rainbow Falls and the Devils Postpile National Monument. Bus access to Mammoth Lakes makes this a popular resupply point.
28 miles ~5,000 ft gain Resupply: Reds Meadow Resort
3
Reds Meadow → Vermilion Valley Resort
Enter deep backcountry as the JMT climbs back into the high Sierra via Silver Pass (10,745 ft). The trail winds through remote terrain alongside Bear Creek, passing stunning lakes. Vermilion Valley Resort on Lake Edison is accessible via a short ferry ride, a beloved hiker hangout with burgers, beer, and hot showers.
31 miles ~6,200 ft gain Resupply: VVR (ferry)
4
VVR → South Fork Kings River
The most awe-inspiring section. Cross Selden Pass (10,900 ft) and descend into the magical Evolution Valley, often called the finest valley in the Sierra. Traverse past Evolution Lake, past the towering Darwin Bench, and over Muir Pass (11,955 ft) with its famous stone emergency hut. Enter Kings Canyon National Park and descend to the South Fork Kings River.
50 miles ~12,000 ft gain Resupply: Muir Trail Ranch (cache)
5
South Fork Kings → Junction Meadow
The pass-heavy central section. Climb Mather Pass (12,100 ft) with its sheer west face, cross Pinchot Pass (12,130 ft) and the dramatic Glen Pass (11,978 ft) descending to the jewel-like Rae Lakes Basin, perhaps the most photographed stretch of the entire JMT. Continue south through Vidette Meadow and over Forester Pass (13,153 ft), the highest point on both the JMT and Pacific Crest Trail.
35 miles ~14,000 ft gain Kearsarge Trailhead access (resupply Bishop)
6
Junction Meadow → Mt. Whitney Summit
The triumphant final push. Climb through Guitar Lake Basin with its stunning Kaweah Peaks backdrop, a favorite final-night camp at 11,460 ft. Ascend Trail Crest (13,777 ft), then the final mile-long ridge traverse to the Mt. Whitney summit at 14,505 ft. Descend 11 miles via the Whitney Trail to Whitney Portal trailhead. Most hikers arrange a car shuttle or taxi to Lone Pine.
46 miles ~11,000 ft gain Finish: Whitney Portal

Expected Challenges

Sustained High Altitude
The majority of the JMT sits above 8,000 feet, with camping regularly above 10,000 ft and ten passes above 10,000 ft. Altitude sickness (AMS) is a real concern, particularly in the first three days. Give yourself at least one night at altitude before starting if coming from sea level. Symptoms include headache, nausea, and fatigue — descend if they worsen.
Highly Competitive Permits
The Yosemite Wilderness Permit for Happy Isles (the traditional southbound start) is one of the hardest permits to score in the country. Applications open in late March via recreation.gov for the upcoming season. Less competitive alternatives include starting from Lyell Canyon, Donohue Pass area, or heading northbound from Whitney Portal. The PCTA permit lottery (January) is separate for PCT combined permits.
River Crossings in Early Season
June and early July hikers may face waist-deep or dangerous river crossings during peak Sierra snowmelt. Evolution Creek, the South Fork Kings River, and multiple other crossings can be hazardous or impassable in high water years. Check current conditions on the JMT Facebook groups and Caltopo snowpack maps before departing. Trekking poles are essential for stability.
Summer Afternoon Thunderstorms
The Sierra Nevada produces intense afternoon thunderstorms from July through early September, especially during summer monsoon patterns. Make it a rule to be off exposed ridges and passes by noon. Lightning is the primary weather danger; exposed passes like Forester, Mather, and Glen are particularly vulnerable. Carry a rain shell regardless of forecast.
Heavy Resupply Carries
The JMT has only 4–5 resupply points along its length (Tuolumne Meadows, Reds Meadow, VVR, Muir Trail Ranch, and Kearsarge Pass exit). Most hikers carry 5–7 days of food between stops, resulting in pack weights of 45–60+ lbs when fully loaded. Ultralight gear, calorie-dense food, and careful bear canister packing are essential. Bear canisters are legally required throughout and must fit all your food.
Mosquitoes — Especially in July
Early-season JMT hikers frequently describe the mosquitoes as "biblical," particularly in the meadows and lake basins around Evolution Valley, Rae Lakes, and Tuolumne Meadows through mid-July. Head nets, permethrin-treated clothing, and DEET are your best tools. The mosquitoes improve dramatically after mid-August.

Overview

The Lost Coast Trail is a 25-mile point-to-point backpacking route along one of the most remote and rugged stretches of coastline in the contiguous United States. Running through the King Range National Conservation Area in Humboldt County, California, the trail travels along dramatic black-sand beaches, past sea stacks and sea lions, and through a roadless wilderness where the mountains rise steeply from the Pacific — forcing the highway inland and earning this stretch its name.


Most hikers walk the Southern King Range section southbound from Mattole Beach to Black Sands Beach near Shelter Cove — 24.6 miles over 2–4 days. This is one of California's most iconic short backpacking trips: easy enough for fit beginners, remote enough to feel like true wilderness, and visually unlike anywhere else on the West Coast. Several sections require timed tidal crossings, so every trip starts with downloading a tide chart.


The trail requires a permit from the BLM (Bureau of Land Management) from May 1 through September 30, with a daily quota of 60 hikers at the Mattole trailhead. Compared to most California wilderness permits, it is relatively easy to obtain. A bear canister is required along the entire route.

Tidal Crossings: What You Need to Know

Check tides before every trip — three sections are impassable at high tide

Three sections of the Lost Coast Trail cannot be safely crossed at high tide: Punta Gorda (around mile 4–5), Sea Lion Gulch (~mile 7), and the Randall Creek / Cooskie Creek area (~mile 14–16). At high tide, waves crash directly against cliff faces with no beach or cliff escape route. Hikers have been swept into the ocean attempting to force these crossings.

Download the NOAA tide chart for Punta Gorda, CA before your trip and plan to cross these sections when the tide is below +2.0 feet. Tide tables are available free at tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov. The BLM Arcata Field Office also posts tide windows on their permit page. Allow 2–3 hours of buffer on either side of low tide for each crossing.

Lost Coast Trail Permit & Planning

Seasonal Permit (May 1 – September 30): Required for all overnight hikers entering from Mattole Beach. Quota is 60 hikers per day at the Mattole trailhead. Permits are issued through recreation.gov starting in early April for the coming season. Unlike the JMT, this permit is relatively easy to get on weekdays — weekend dates fill faster. Walk-up permits are sometimes available the day before at the BLM Arcata office.

No Permit Required (October 1 – April 30): Off-season hiking is permit-free but conditions are significantly more challenging. Winter storms produce massive surf that can make tidal crossings genuinely dangerous. If you go off-season, you must check surf reports in addition to tide charts.

Direction: Most hikers go southbound from Mattole Beach to Black Sands Beach — the prevailing wind at your back makes this the most comfortable direction. Northbound (Shelter Cove to Mattole) is possible but you walk into the wind. A car shuttle or the Lost Coast Shuttle service connects the two trailheads (~2 hours apart by road).

Bear Canister: Legally required for the entire route. There are no bear boxes at campsites. The BearVault BV500 or similar 650+ cubic inch canister fits 3–4 days of food. Do not hang food — coastal black bears have learned to find hung bags.

Water: Freshwater sources are plentiful — nearly every creek you cross is drinkable after treatment. Bring a filter or purification tablets. Do not rely on the ocean or tidal pools. Camp at least 100 feet from any water source per BLM regulations.

Campfires: Permitted on the beach below the high-tide line, but often restricted during high fire danger (check current restrictions at the BLM Arcata Field Office). Bring a stove regardless.

Day-by-Day Route Breakdown

1
Mattole Beach → Punta Gorda / Cooskie Creek
Begin at the Mattole Beach trailhead (end of Lighthouse Road, Petrolia, CA — 3 hours from San Francisco). Head south along wide black-sand beaches past the Mattole River mouth. The first major tidal section is at Punta Gorda near the historic lighthouse at mile 4.5 — do not attempt within 2 hours of high tide. Continue through Sea Lion Gulch (~mi 7) where sea lions haul out on the rocks. Good first camps at Randall Creek (~mi 8) or Cooskie Creek (~mi 15).
8–15 miles 500 ft gain 2 tidal crossings
2
Cooskie Creek → Big Flat
The middle section of the trail passes through Spanish Flat and includes the third tidal crossing at the Cooskie Creek / Miller Flat area (~mi 15–16) — again time carefully. The walk opens up on long gravel and cobble beaches with dramatic views of the King Range rising 4,000 feet directly from the sea. Big Flat (~mi 19) is the most popular and spacious campsite on the entire trail, with a large flat sandy camping area, reliable freshwater from Flat Creek, and stunning sunset views.
4–11 miles 800 ft gain Big Flat campsite
3
Big Flat → Black Sands Beach (Shelter Cove)
The final stretch follows more open beach and headland sections. The trail briefly climbs above the coast at Whale Gulch, offering panoramic views of the entire coastline. Gitchell Creek (~mi 22) is the last reliable water source and a pleasant final camp for those wanting a sunrise finish. The trail ends at Black Sands Beach, the iconic black volcanic sand beach at the edge of Shelter Cove — a small community with a general store, airstrip, and several vacation rentals. Arrange a shuttle or taxi back to Mattole (2 hours by road).
6–10 miles 600 ft gain Finish: Black Sands Beach

Expected Challenges

Tidal Crossings — The #1 Safety Risk
Three sections cannot be safely crossed at high tide. This is not a theoretical concern — hikers have died on the Lost Coast by attempting tidal sections during dangerous conditions. Download the NOAA tide chart for Punta Gorda, CA before you go. Plan your schedule backward from each crossing, building in a 2-hour buffer. Most experienced hikers time Punta Gorda as a morning crossing within 2 hours of low tide.
Cobble and Beach Walking Is Slower Than You Think
The Lost Coast Trail is rated "moderate" but 24.6 miles on black cobble beaches is far more physically demanding than 24.6 miles on a dirt trail. Expect your pace to drop to 1.5–2 mph on cobble sections. Budget extra time on day one in particular — most hikers underestimate how tiring beach walking feels on loose rock. Trail runners and light hikers report ankle fatigue by midday.
Complete Remoteness — No Cell Service, No Bailout
The Lost Coast has zero cell coverage along its entire length. There is no road access mid-route. If something goes wrong — injury, weather change, medical emergency — your only options are self-rescue back to Mattole, forward to Shelter Cove, or activating a PLB or satellite communicator. Bring a Garmin inReach or SPOT device on any trip here. Tell someone your exact route and check-in schedule.
Unpredictable Weather and Fog
Northern California coastal weather can shift rapidly. Dense fog is common, especially in summer mornings. Wind can be relentless — southbound is easier because you have wind at your back. Afternoon fog often clears for stunning sunsets. Pack a rain shell regardless of the forecast. In winter and early spring, storm swells of 20+ feet can temporarily flood beach sections even at low tide.
Black Bears Habituated to Hikers
Lost Coast black bears are active, bold, and have learned to associate backpackers with food. A bear canister is legally required — not optional. Do not hang food: bears here are experienced tree-climbers. Set up camp well away from any food smells, and keep a clean camp. Bear encounters are common at popular campsites like Big Flat; don't be alarmed, but be firm and loud if a bear approaches camp at night.
Shuttle Logistics
Mattole Beach and Black Sands Beach are about 2 hours apart by road. You need a car shuttle, two cars, or a hired shuttle service. The Lost Coast Shuttle (lostcoastshuttle.com) is the most commonly used service — book it early as it fills weeks in advance for summer weekends. Without a shuttle plan, you'll end up leaving a car at one end and having no way back.

Frequently Asked Questions

How hard is the Lost Coast Trail?
It's rated moderate in terms of total elevation gain (~3,000 ft over 25 miles), but the difficulty is higher than that number suggests because the majority of hiking is on loose cobble beach. Most fit hikers with backpacking experience can complete it in 3 days. Plan for 1.5–2 mph on cobble sections, not your usual trail pace. The mandatory tidal crossings add a logistical layer that makes it more serious than a typical moderate trail.
Do I need a permit for the Lost Coast Trail?
Yes — from May 1 through September 30, all overnight hikers starting at Mattole Beach need a permit. The quota is 60 hikers per day and permits are available at recreation.gov. From October 1 through April 30, no permit is required. The permit is per-person and must be purchased before arriving — no walk-ups at the trailhead during permit season.
What are the tidal crossings and how do I plan them?
Three sections cannot be safely passed at high tide: Punta Gorda (~mi 4.5), Sea Lion Gulch (~mi 7), and the Cooskie Creek area (~mi 15). Download the NOAA tide table for Punta Gorda, CA and plan to cross within ±2 hours of low tide, when the tide is below +2.0 feet. Most hikers build their entire day-by-day itinerary around the tide windows. The BLM website also posts crossing guidance for the current season.
How do I get from Black Sands Beach back to Mattole?
The two trailheads are roughly 2 hours apart by road. Options: (1) Lost Coast Shuttle — the dedicated shuttle service runs seasonally; book well in advance for summer weekends. (2) Two-car shuttle — leave one car at each end before starting. (3) Hitchhike from Shelter Cove — it's a small community, not always reliable. Most hikers use the shuttle service as it's affordable and solves the logistics entirely.
Is a bear canister required on the Lost Coast?
Yes — a bear canister is legally required for all overnight camping in the King Range NCA. The BLM enforces this strictly. Lost Coast bears are active and experienced at raiding food that isn't properly stored. Popular options include the BearVault BV500 (650 ci, fits 3–4 days of food) or the Counter Assault Bear Keg. Note that hanging food does not satisfy the regulation — only a certified hard-sided canister is accepted.
Can beginners do the Lost Coast Trail?
Yes, with preparation. The Lost Coast is a reasonable first multi-day backpacking trip for fit hikers who take the planning seriously — particularly the tides. It's shorter than most wilderness trips (25 miles), lower-elevation than alpine routes, and water is abundant. The main requirements: study the tide chart before you go, carry a bear canister, bring a reliable shelter, and carry a satellite communicator given the lack of cell coverage. A 3-night itinerary is more comfortable than 2 nights for most beginners.
What is the best campsite on the Lost Coast Trail?
Big Flat (~mile 19) is widely considered the best campsite on the trail — a large flat sandy area at a creek crossing with space for many tents, reliable water, and unobstructed sunset views over the Pacific. It's typically a second-night camp for hikers doing 3 days southbound. Gitchell Creek (~mi 22) is the best spot for a final night before an early finish the next morning. Randall Creek (~mi 8) is a comfortable early stop for hikers who arrive late at Mattole on day one.
What's the difference between the Southern and Northern Lost Coast?
The Southern King Range section (Mattole to Black Sands Beach, 24.6 mi) is what most people mean when they say "the Lost Coast Trail" — it's accessible, well-documented, has the permit system, and passes through the most dramatic coastal scenery. The Northern section runs through the Sinkyone Wilderness State Park (Usal Beach to Needle Rock, ~25 mi) and is wilder, less maintained, more overgrown, and receives far fewer visitors. The Northern section has no permit requirement and no quota, but requires more route-finding and has no shuttle services.
